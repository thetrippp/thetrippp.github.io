Historical & Technical Context

The universe began as a hot, dense soup of hydrogen and helium. The very first stars, or Population III stars, formed from this pristine material approximately 100 million years after the Big Bang. Their powerful radiation and subsequent explosive deaths (supernovae) were the universe's first factories for heavier elements and profoundly influenced the surrounding gas, which in turn affected how the first galaxies coalesced. Modern galaxies, by contrast, are rich in metals, which changes how gas cools, how stars form, and how galactic structures evolve. Directly observing these distant, primordial Population III stars is beyond the capabilities of current telescopes, even the James Webb Space Telescope. Therefore, studying "local analogs"—stars in nearby galaxies that are exceptionally metal-poor and share characteristics with these ancient stars—is a crucial workaround. The Hubble Space Telescope, a joint mission of NASA and ESA, with its Cosmic Origins Spectrograph (COS), is uniquely capable of detecting the faint ultraviolet (UV) light emitted by these hot O stars. This UV light carries distinct spectral signatures of their elemental composition, allowing astronomers to accurately gauge their metal content and infer their primordial nature. The TEMPOS survey focuses on understanding the feedback mechanisms—like powerful stellar winds and intense radiation—from these early-type stars, which would have regulated gas collapse and star formation rates in the infant galaxies, thereby dictating their morphology.
