While closely related to the famous P versus NP millennium prize problem, ETH is significantly stronger: it posits that solving NP-hard problems fundamentally requires algorithms whose runtime scales as an exponential function of the input size, establishing theoretical speed limits for cryptography, optimization, and computer algorithms. Frontier AI labs have increasingly transitioned from standard generative transformer pre-training toward inference-time compute scaling and deep reinforcement learning pipelines designed for formalized theorem proving (using interactive proof assistants like Lean and Isabelle). By systematically traversing vast proof trees and uncovering non-linear mathematical shortcuts that human researchers missed, these models are generating structural arguments that circumvent classical algorithmic barriers, forcing a radical reassessment of how asymptotic complexity is evaluated in theoretical mathematics.

On the academic and validation side are theoretical computer scientists at institutions like MIT, whose motivation is intellectual rigor and the preservation of mathematical truth; they face the monumental burden of rigorously decompiling, understanding, and peer-reviewing machine-generated proofs whose intermediate logical steps often lack intuitive human narrative. Observing with intense trepidation are cryptographers and national security agencies, who understand that any theoretical collapse of complexity classes like ETH directly endangers the mathematical assumptions underpinning asymmetric encryption, zero-knowledge proofs, and global cryptographic security.
